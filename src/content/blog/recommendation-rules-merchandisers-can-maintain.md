Recommendation rules are often written once, by whoever set up the system, and then left alone. Months later nobody remembers why a rule exists, what it was meant to achieve or whether it still makes sense. The result is a pile of logic that the merchandising team is afraid to touch.

It does not have to work that way. Rules can be written so that the people closest to the products can read them, test them and change them with confidence. This article covers the habits that make that possible.

## Start with the reason

Every rule should begin with a single sentence that explains why it exists. Not how it works, but what problem it solves.

Good examples:

- "Customers who buy a desk lamp often need bulbs, and we want them to find bulbs without searching."
- "First-time buyers in the home category should see our starter range, not our premium range."

A clear reason helps in two ways. It stops rules from being created without a purpose, and it gives the next person a way to judge whether the rule is still worth keeping.

## Keep each rule small

It is tempting to write one large rule that handles many cases. "If the customer is in segment A or B, and the product is in category C, D or E, and the season is winter, show these twelve items." Rules like this are hard to read and harder to change safely.

Smaller rules are easier to maintain:

- One rule per clear situation.
- One rule type per rule: customer-specific, segment-based, related-product or complementary-product.
- A short list of products or a clear product criterion, not both mixed together.

If two small rules overlap, that is usually easier to manage than one large rule with hidden branches.

## Use names a person would use

Rules and segments should be named in the language the team already uses. "Home and living interest" is better than "seg_hl_02". "Complementary: lamp to bulbs" is better than "rule 117".

A useful test is to read the rule name aloud in a team meeting. If people know what it means without opening it, the name is doing its job.

## Prefer criteria over hand-picked lists

When a rule shows "related products", it is usually better to define them by shared attributes than to pick them one by one. A rule that says "show other products in the same category and brand" keeps working when new products are added. A hand-picked list needs updating every time the catalog changes.

There are exceptions. Complementary products often need a person's judgement, because "used together" is not always visible in attributes. In those cases a short, curated list is fine, as long as someone owns it.

## Decide what happens when a rule has nothing to show

Rules will sometimes find no products: an item is out of stock, a segment is empty or a category has been retired. Decide in advance what should happen.

Common options:

1. Fall back to a broader rule, such as related products in the same category.
2. Show nothing, which is better than showing something irrelevant.
3. Show a small, regularly refreshed list of popular items.

Writing the fallback down as part of the rule avoids surprises later.

## Record changes

Every change to a rule should leave a trace: who changed it, when and what changed. This is not about blame. It is about being able to answer the question "why did recommendations look different last Tuesday?"

A change record also makes it safer to experiment. If a new version of a rule performs worse, you can see exactly what the previous version was and restore it.

## Review rules on a schedule

Rules age. Products are discontinued, seasons change and customer groups shift. A simple review routine keeps the rule set healthy:

- **Monthly:** check rules with very few impressions. They may be targeting a segment or product that no longer exists.
- **Quarterly:** read every rule's reason. Remove the ones whose reason no longer applies.
- **After catalog changes:** check rules that reference specific products or categories that moved.

Reports on impressions, clicks and conversions per rule make these reviews much faster, because they show which rules are doing work and which are idle.

## A template to start from

When creating a new rule, it can help to fill in the same fields every time:

- **Name:** short and readable.
- **Reason:** one sentence.
- **Type:** customer-specific, segment-based, related-product or complementary-product.
- **Applies to:** the segment or customer criteria.
- **Shows:** the products or product criteria.
- **Fallback:** what happens if nothing matches.
- **Owner:** who keeps it up to date.
- **Review date:** when to check it again.

## Summary

Maintainable rules are short, named in plain language, built on clear criteria and backed by a stated reason. Each change is recorded, and the whole set is reviewed on a schedule. None of this needs specialist skills. It needs a shared format and a little discipline, and it gives the merchandising team real control over what customers are shown.
