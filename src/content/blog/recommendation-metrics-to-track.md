Once recommendations are live, the next question is whether they help. There are many possible measures, but three cover most of what a retail team needs at the start: impressions, clicks and conversions. Read together, they show where a recommendation is working and where it is not.

## The three metrics

### Impressions

An impression is counted when a recommendation is shown. If a row of three products appears on a page, that may count as one impression for the placement and one for each product, depending on how your reporting is set up.

Impressions tell you **reach**. A rule with very few impressions is not doing much, whatever its other numbers look like. It may target a segment that is too small, or products that are often out of stock.

### Clicks

A click is counted when someone selects a recommended product.

Clicks tell you **interest**. On their own they say little, because a placement shown more often will usually get more clicks. They become meaningful when compared with impressions.

### Conversions

A conversion is counted when a recommended product is bought, usually within a defined window after it was clicked or shown.

Conversions tell you **outcome**. They are the closest of the three to business value, but they are also the slowest to build up and the easiest to misread.

## Reading them together

The ratios between the three numbers are often more useful than the numbers themselves.

- **Click rate** is clicks divided by impressions. It shows how appealing a recommendation is to the people who see it.
- **Conversion rate from clicks** is conversions divided by clicks. It shows whether interest turns into purchases.
- **Conversion rate from impressions** is conversions divided by impressions. It gives an overall view of how much a placement contributes.

Some typical patterns:

1. **High impressions, low clicks.** The recommendation is seen but ignored. The products may not fit the context, or the placement may be easy to overlook.
2. **Good clicks, low conversions.** People are curious but do not buy. Check price, availability and whether the product page answers their questions.
3. **Low impressions, strong rates.** The rule works when it fires but rarely fires. Consider widening the segment or criteria.
4. **Steady across the board.** The rule is doing its job. Leave it alone and look elsewhere.

## Choosing a period

Numbers swing from day to day. A fair comparison needs a period long enough to smooth that out and short enough to be relevant. Many teams start with weekly or monthly views and compare the same period before and after a change.

When comparing periods, keep an eye on outside factors such as seasonal demand, promotions or stock changes. A drop in conversions during a week when a key product sold out says more about stock than about the rule.

## Comparing rules fairly

Different rules sit in different places and reach different audiences, so their raw numbers are not directly comparable. A basket-page complementary rule will usually behave differently from a homepage segment rule.

Fairer comparisons include:

- The same rule before and after a change.
- Two versions of a rule shown to similar groups over the same period.
- Rules of the same type in the same placement.

## Common traps

### Counting the wrong thing

Make sure everyone agrees on definitions. Does an impression mean the row loaded, or that it was visible on screen? Does a conversion count any purchase in the session, or only the recommended product? Write the definitions down and keep them stable.

### Crediting recommendations for every sale

A customer who clicked a recommendation may have bought the product anyway. Conversions show association, not proof that the recommendation caused the sale. Treat them as a strong signal, not a final verdict.

### Reacting too quickly

Small numbers move a lot. A rule with ten impressions and two conversions looks excellent, but it may simply be lucky. Wait for enough data before drawing conclusions.

### Looking only at averages

An average across all rules hides the detail. A few strong rules can cover for many weak ones. Reports per rule, per segment and per placement give a clearer picture.

## A simple routine

A light reporting routine is often enough:

- **Weekly:** scan impressions, clicks and conversions per rule for anything unusual.
- **Monthly:** review click and conversion rates for each placement, and note rules to adjust or retire.
- **After any change:** compare the same rule over equal periods before and after.

Exporting these reports lets you share them with the wider team and keep a history of what changed and when.

## Summary

Impressions show reach, clicks show interest and conversions show outcome. Each on its own can mislead. Together, and read over sensible periods, they give a clear and honest view of whether recommendations are helping customers find the right product.
