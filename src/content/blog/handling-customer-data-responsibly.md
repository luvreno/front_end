Every personalised suggestion relies on information about a customer: what they bought, what they said they like, which group they belong to. That information is valuable to the business and personal to the customer. Handling it well is not only a legal matter. It shapes whether customers trust the suggestions they see.

This article sets out practical habits for retail teams that use customer data for recommendations. It is general guidance, not legal advice. Requirements vary by country and situation, so check specifics with a qualified advisor.

## Collect what you need, and know why

The simplest way to reduce risk is to hold less data. Before adding a new field to a customer profile, ask:

- What decision will this information support?
- Is there a simpler signal that would do the same job?
- How long do we need to keep it?

A preference such as "interested in home and living" is often more useful for recommendations than detailed personal attributes, and it is far less sensitive. Purchase history and stated preferences cover most practical needs.

Writing down the purpose of each field also helps later. When someone asks why a piece of information is stored, there is a clear answer.

## Be open with customers

Customers are more comfortable with personalization when they understand it. Good practice includes:

- **A clear privacy notice** that explains what is collected, why and for how long.
- **Plain language about recommendations**, for example that suggestions are based on past purchases and stated preferences.
- **Easy ways to update preferences**, so customers can correct or remove information.
- **Honest consent choices** for optional tracking, with refusing as easy as accepting.

Openness also means not overstating what a system does. If recommendations come from rules and recorded data, say so. There is no need to imply more.

## Control who can see what

Not everyone on a team needs access to every customer record. Role-based access sets sensible limits:

- Merchandisers may need product data and aggregated reports, but not individual contact details.
- Customer service staff may need individual profiles to help with orders.
- Administrators manage accounts and permissions.

Limiting access reduces the chance of mistakes and makes it easier to show that information is handled carefully. Review roles when people join, change jobs or leave.

## Keep a record of changes

Audit records answer the question "who changed what, and when?" For customer data and personalization, useful things to record include:

- Changes to customer profiles.
- Changes to segment criteria.
- Changes to recommendation rules.
- Exports of reports that contain customer information.

These records support investigations if something goes wrong, help with requests from customers about their data, and give the team confidence that changes can be traced.

## Use labels, not identities, wherever possible

Many tasks do not need to know who a customer is. Testing a rule, reviewing a segment or presenting a report can often use anonymous labels such as "Customer 1042" and segment names. Keeping names and contact details out of everyday views reduces exposure without reducing usefulness.

The same applies to demos and training. Use sample data, never real customer records.

## Handle exports with care

Reports and exports are where data most often leaks. A spreadsheet sent by email or saved to a shared drive can outlive its purpose by years. Some sensible habits:

- Export aggregated data where possible, rather than individual records.
- Limit who can export customer-level data.
- Record exports, and agree how long exported files are kept.
- Delete local copies once the task is done.

## Plan for retention and deletion

Data that is no longer needed is a liability. Set retention periods for each type of information and apply them. For example, you might keep purchase history for a defined period for recommendations and reporting, then remove or anonymise it.

Make sure there is a process for handling requests to access, correct or delete personal data, and that it covers every place the data lives, including backups and exports where practical.

## Be careful with sensitive inferences

Some conclusions drawn from purchase data can be sensitive, even if the raw data is not. A pattern of purchases might suggest something about health, family situation or beliefs. As a general rule, avoid creating segments or rules that infer sensitive characteristics, and review new segments with this in mind.

## A checklist for new personalization work

Before launching a new segment or rule, ask:

- Does it use only the data it needs?
- Would a customer find the reason for a suggestion reasonable if they saw it?
- Is access to the underlying data limited to the right roles?
- Are changes recorded?
- Could it reveal or imply something sensitive?

## Summary

Responsible personalization rests on a few steady habits: collect what you need, explain it plainly, limit access, record changes and delete what you no longer use. These habits protect customers, reduce risk for the business and make recommendations easier to trust.
