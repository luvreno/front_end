Most online stores have a row of suggested products somewhere on the page. It might be labelled "You may also like", "Popular right now" or "Customers also viewed". In many stores that row shows the same handful of items to every visitor, whatever they came for. It fills the space, but it rarely earns attention.

This article looks at why generic recommendations tend to underperform, and at the practical steps a retail team can take to make suggestions feel relevant without building anything exotic.

## What makes a recommendation generic

A recommendation is generic when nothing about the person seeing it, or the product they are looking at, changed what was shown. Common examples include:

- A fixed list of best sellers placed on every product page.
- A "new arrivals" carousel that ignores the category the shopper is browsing.
- A homepage banner promoting the same item to first-time visitors and loyal customers alike.

None of these are wrong in themselves. Best sellers sell for a reason, and new arrivals need exposure. The problem starts when they are the only kind of suggestion a store makes.

## Why shoppers tune them out

### They repeat what the shopper has already seen

If a returning customer sees the same five products every visit, the row quickly becomes part of the background. People are good at ignoring things that never change. A suggestion that was interesting on the first visit is wallpaper by the fourth.

### They ignore context

Someone looking at a winter coat is in a different frame of mind from someone browsing kitchen storage. A suggestion that ignores the current page asks the shopper to switch topics, and most will not bother. Relevance often comes less from knowing who the person is and more from noticing what they are doing right now.

### They do not explain themselves

"Recommended for you" is a promise. When the products underneath clearly have nothing to do with the shopper, the label works against the store. It signals that the system is not really paying attention, and it can make people trust the other suggestions less.

### They treat every customer the same

A first purchase and a fifteenth purchase are very different moments. A new customer may need reassurance and simple entry products. A regular customer may be ready for accessories, refills or a step up in range. One list cannot serve both.

## What relevant looks like in practice

Relevance does not require guessing at hidden intentions. In most retail settings it comes from a few plain sources of information that teams already hold:

1. **The product being viewed.** Its category, brand and attributes say a lot about what else might be useful.
2. **The customer's recorded preferences.** If a profile lists a preferred category or size, that is a direct signal.
3. **Purchase history.** What someone bought before shapes what makes sense next, including what they no longer need.
4. **Segment membership.** Groups such as "frequent buyers" or "first purchase" let a team set sensible defaults for many customers at once.

When these are kept in one place, a team can write simple rules that use them. A rule might say: for customers in the home and living segment who bought a desk lamp, show plant pots and mugs. That is not clever, but it is relevant, and the team can read it and change it.

## Rules a team can own

One reason stores fall back on generic lists is that anything more specific feels like it needs a data science project. It does not have to. A small set of readable rules, maintained by the people who know the products, covers a lot of ground:

- **Customer-specific rules** use preferences recorded on a profile.
- **Segment-based rules** set suggestions for a whole group.
- **Related-product rules** show items that share a category, brand or attribute with the current product.
- **Complementary-product rules** show items that are used together, such as a phone case with a phone.

Because each rule has a clear reason behind it, the team can explain why a product appeared. That makes it far easier to fix a suggestion that looks odd, and to retire one that has stopped making sense.

## Keep a fallback, but use it last

Generic lists still have a place. When nothing is known about a visitor and the page offers little context, a short list of popular items is a reasonable default. The difference is ordering. Specific rules should be tried first, and the generic list should only fill gaps.

It also helps to keep fallbacks fresh. Rotating the list, excluding items the customer already bought and respecting stock levels all reduce the "wallpaper" effect.

## Measure before and after

Changing recommendations is only useful if you can tell whether it helped. Three numbers give a reasonable first view:

- **Impressions:** how often a recommendation was shown.
- **Clicks:** how often someone selected it.
- **Conversions:** how often that led to a purchase.

Comparing these for generic and rule-based suggestions over the same period shows where the effort is paying off. It also exposes rules that look sensible on paper but are rarely clicked, which are good candidates for a rewrite.

## A short checklist

Before adding another "you may also like" row, it is worth asking:

- Does this suggestion change based on the product or the customer?
- Could someone on the team explain why each item appears?
- Are items the customer already owns excluded where that makes sense?
- Is there a way to see impressions, clicks and conversions for this placement?

If the answer to most of these is no, the row is probably generic. The fix is rarely a bigger system. It is usually better organised customer and product information, a few clear rules and a habit of checking the results.
