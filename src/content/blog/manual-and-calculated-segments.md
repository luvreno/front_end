Segments are one of the most useful tools a retail team has. They let you treat groups of customers differently without writing a separate plan for every person. But not all segments are built the same way, and mixing up the two main kinds is a common source of confusion.

This article explains the difference between manual and calculated segments, when each one fits, and how to keep them from drifting apart over time.

## Two ways to build a segment

### Manual segments

A manual segment is a list. Someone on the team decides which customers belong in it and adds them directly. Membership only changes when a person changes it.

Typical examples:

- A list of customers invited to a private launch.
- Wholesale or trade accounts that a sales team manages.
- Customers who asked to be part of a product testing group.

### Calculated segments

A calculated segment is a rule. You describe the criteria, and membership is worked out from the data. When the data changes, membership changes with it.

Typical examples:

- Customers who placed five or more orders.
- Customers whose profile lists home and living as an interest.
- Customers whose first purchase was in the last thirty days.

The key difference is where the truth lives. For a manual segment, the truth is the list itself. For a calculated segment, the truth is the criteria, and the list is simply the current result.

## When a manual segment is the right choice

Manual segments work best when membership depends on a decision rather than a fact in your data.

Use one when:

- **Membership is a relationship.** Trade accounts, partners or VIP guests are chosen, not detected.
- **The group is small and deliberate.** A list of twenty customers for a pilot is easier to manage by hand than to describe with criteria.
- **The reason is not recorded anywhere else.** If the only place the information exists is in someone's head or an email thread, a manual list captures it.

The risk with manual segments is that they go stale. People join, leave or change, and nobody updates the list. A good habit is to give every manual segment an owner and a review date.

## When a calculated segment is the right choice

Calculated segments are the better choice when membership follows from information you already store.

Use one when:

- **The criteria are clear.** "Five or more orders" or "interested in home and living" can be checked against records without judgement.
- **The group is large or changes often.** Keeping a list of frequent buyers up to date by hand would be slow and error prone.
- **You want consistency.** A calculated segment treats every customer the same way, which makes results easier to compare over time.

The risk with calculated segments is that the criteria can be wrong or out of date. If "frequent buyer" was defined years ago, it may no longer match how the business thinks about loyalty. Criteria need the same periodic review as lists do.

## Keep the two apart

Problems tend to appear when a team edits a calculated segment by hand. Someone removes a customer from "frequent buyers" because of a complaint, or adds a customer who "should" qualify. The next time the criteria are applied, the manual change is either lost or, worse, silently kept in a way nobody can explain.

A cleaner approach is:

1. **Never hand-edit a calculated segment.** If the result is wrong, fix the criteria or the underlying data.
2. **Use a separate manual segment for exceptions.** For example, keep "exclude from promotions" as its own list, and let rules check both.
3. **Label segments clearly.** Anyone looking at a segment should be able to tell at a glance whether it is manual or calculated.

Tools that show the type of each segment, and record changes to criteria, make this much easier to follow.

## Combining them in recommendation rules

Most useful rules draw on both kinds. A complementary-product rule might apply to a calculated segment such as "bought a desk lamp in the last sixty days", while a manual segment such as "trade accounts" is excluded because they buy in bulk through a different channel.

When writing rules like this, it helps to note which segments are inputs and why. That way, if a segment's definition changes, you can find the rules that depend on it.

## Questions to ask before creating a segment

- Is membership a decision or a fact?
- Who will keep this segment accurate, and how often?
- If it is calculated, are the criteria written down in plain language?
- If it is manual, is there a review date?
- Which rules or reports will use it?

## Summary

Manual segments hold decisions. Calculated segments hold criteria. Both are valuable, and most teams need both. The trouble starts when one is treated like the other. Keep them clearly labelled, keep exceptions in their own lists, and review both kinds on a schedule. Your segments will stay accurate, and the rules built on top of them will be easier to trust.
