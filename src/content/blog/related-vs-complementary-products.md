Two of the most common recommendation types are easy to confuse. Both show other products next to the one a customer is looking at. Both can be labelled "you might also like". But they answer different questions, and using the wrong one in the wrong place can quietly work against you.

## The short version

- **Related products** are alternatives. They answer the question: "Is there something like this that suits me better?"
- **Complementary products** are companions. They answer the question: "What else do I need to go with this?"

A different colour of the same sneaker is related. Laces, socks or a shoe cleaner are complementary.

## Related products

Related products share characteristics with the item being viewed. They usually have the same category and similar attributes, and often the same brand or price range.

### When they help

Related products are most useful early in a shopping journey, when the customer is still choosing. Someone comparing desk lamps benefits from seeing other desk lamps with different heights, finishes or prices. It saves them from going back to search.

They also help when an item is unavailable. If a customer's preferred size is out of stock, a closely related product keeps them from leaving empty handed.

### When they get in the way

Once a customer has decided, related products can become a distraction. Showing five other lamps on the basket page invites second thoughts at the exact moment the customer is ready to buy. That is rarely what the store wants.

### How to define them

Related products are usually best described with criteria rather than picked by hand:

- Same category.
- One or two shared attributes, such as material or style.
- A similar price band.
- Available to buy now.

Criteria like these keep working as the catalog grows.

## Complementary products

Complementary products are used together with the item being viewed. They do not compete with it. They make it more useful.

### When they help

Complementary products shine once the main decision is made: on the product page after a size is chosen, in the basket, or in a follow-up message after purchase. A customer who has decided on a lamp may well need a bulb. A customer buying a plant pot may want a saucer or potting mix.

They are also valuable for customers who have bought before. Someone who purchased a coffee machine some time ago is a natural audience for filters or cleaning tablets.

### When they get in the way

Complementary suggestions fail when the link is weak. Showing a phone case next to a notebook because both were bought in the same order once does not mean they belong together. The connection should make sense to a person reading it.

They can also feel pushy if there are too many. Two or three well chosen companions are more helpful than a long list.

### How to define them

Complementary relationships are harder to describe with attributes alone. "Used together" often depends on knowledge the product data does not hold. Common approaches:

- A curated list per product or per category, maintained by someone who knows the range.
- Category pairings, such as "lamps go with bulbs" or "pots go with saucers".
- Patterns in purchase history that a person has reviewed and confirmed.

Whichever approach you use, write down the reason for each pairing so it can be checked later.

## Placing them on the page

A simple guide that works for many stores:

| Placement | Better choice | Why |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Category or search results | Related | The customer is still comparing |
| Product page, before choosing | Related | Helps them find the right version |
| Product page, after choosing | Complementary | Helps complete the purchase |
| Basket | Complementary | Adds value without reopening the decision |
| After purchase | Complementary | Supports use of what they bought |

This is a starting point, not a rule. Your own reports on impressions, clicks and conversions will show what works for your customers.

## Combining them with segments

Both types become more useful when combined with customer information. A related-product rule for a first-time buyer might favour entry-level options. A complementary rule for a frequent buyer might skip accessories they have already purchased.

Keeping these as separate, clearly named rules makes them easier to manage than one rule trying to handle every case.

## Checking you have it right

A quick review question for any recommendation placement: "If the customer clicks this, does it replace what they were looking at, or add to it?"

If it replaces, it is related. If it adds, it is complementary. Then ask whether that is what you want at that point in the journey. Getting this right is one of the simplest ways to make recommendations feel helpful instead of noisy.
